\begin{abstract}
Additional self-play improves the match-winning performance of published
Koi-Koi Transformers while preserving their architecture and greedy inference.
We formulate a stochastic policy over the pretrained action scores, train it
with player-relative match returns, and implement parallel simulation in a
shared C++/CUDA engine. Each adapted model uses 8.52 million additional decisions.
On 10,000 held-out duplicate deals, two independently adapted models achieve
51.480\% and 51.445\% draw-adjusted wins against the original assisted win-oriented
RL-WP policy; both 95\% intervals exclude 50\%. A colder exploration setting
with a reduced learning rate performs worse in paired development comparisons.
Against the point-oriented RL-Point policy, win-rate improvement is inconsistent
across the two models, and expected points favor RL-Point. This objective-dependent
result identifies a remaining challenge: converting strong round-level scoring
into reliable match-level decisions. The canonical adapted agent averages
1.585 ms per CPU decision. The study provides a reproducible implementation,
controlled adaptation results, and retained negative experiments that support
further work on combining win-oriented and point-oriented play.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Koi-Koi rewards both collecting valuable card combinations and deciding when
to stop. Continuing after a scoring opportunity can increase the gain, but it
also gives the opponent another chance to score. Across a multi-round match,
the same hand can therefore call for different decisions depending on the
score and the number of rounds remaining. Maximizing expected points and
maximizing the chance of winning are related but distinct learning objectives.

Guan et al. demonstrated Transformer-based Koi-Koi agents trained for these
two objectives: RL-WP for wins and RL-Point for points
\cite{guan2023koikoi,pinnedkoikoi}. Their released weights provide a strong
starting point for studying further improvement. We ask whether terminal
match rewards can adapt these existing representations efficiently, and where
such adaptation still falls short.

Our method converts the pretrained action scores into a stochastic policy and
optimizes it by self-play with PPO \cite{schulman2017ppo}. A separate critic
estimates match returns from the acting player's public observation. Returns
retain future-round value and change sign only when the acting player changes.
A shared CPU/GPU simulator makes these updates practical while preserving the
published feature and scoring conventions. Deployment retains greedy action
selection and requires no search.

The study makes three contributions. First, it establishes a replicated
improvement over the published win-oriented policy using 8.52 million additional
decisions per model. Second, a paired comparison of two exploration and
learning-rate settings identifies a better adaptation setting, while a separate
opponent comparison exposes the remaining gap between wins and expected points.
Third, the implementation connects reference rules, native simulation, exact
training restart, and paired match evaluation in a reusable research system.
These are contributions in adaptation, empirical analysis, and infrastructure;
PPO and Transformer architectures are inherited methods.

The result against RL-Point motivates a second question: can a small public-state
controller choose useful actions from differently trained experts more effectively
than continuing to update the entire policy? We specify this extension in
Section~\ref{sec:arbitration}. Its comparative experiment is ongoing; the measured
results reported here remain those of the completed adaptation study.

\section{Related work}
\subsection{Learning to play Koi-Koi}
Early Koi-Koi learning systems already compared policy gradients with value
learning. Sato and Ikeda trained neural policies and Neural Fitted Q Iteration
from 268 game features against a rule-based opponent \cite{sato2017koikoi}.
Their experiments used a simplified scoring variant, so those reported scores
are not a common benchmark for the present eight-round game.
Nagamine and Suzuki constructed fast-finishing and blocking strategies by
changing Monte Carlo rollout rewards \cite{nagamine2021strategy}. Their setup
disabled koi-koi continuation, and they proposed switching scoring and
fast-finishing strategies across a match as future work. This anticipates
the strategic motivation for combining objectives. Our extension studies a
learned selector over released neural experts with complete-match supervision
and fixed hybrid controls, including both card play and continuation decisions.

Guan et al. use phase-specific Transformers to represent card relations and
learn action preferences through reinforcement learning
\cite{guan2023koikoi}. Their two reward objectives produce the RL-WP and
RL-Point agents used here. We reuse their published actors and input conventions,
then optimize an explicit sampling policy with terminal match rewards. In
the released RL-WP training code, sampled actions receive a round-ending target
from a continuation-win table indexed by the next round, resulting score and
dealer role \cite{pinnedkoikoi}. Our targets retain the actual terminal match
outcome across round boundaries. This
makes the comparison an adaptation study: the inherited representations are a
shared dependency, while the additional optimization and its measured effects
are the subject of our experiments. Evaluating both published objectives is
essential because an improvement against RL-WP need not transfer to RL-Point.

\subsection{Self-play and equilibrium-oriented learning}
Self-play methods differ in the policies they train against and the guarantees
they seek. Neural Fictitious Self-Play combines reinforcement learning of
best responses with supervised learning of average strategies
\cite{heinrich2016nfsp}. Policy-Space Response Oracles maintain a population
and compute responses to mixtures selected through an empirical game
\cite{lanctot2017psro}. Both approaches address the strategic interaction among
policies beyond simply training against the latest self.

Our adaptation instead uses current-policy self-play and measures performance
against fixed external opponents. The distinction matters in imperfect-information
games: counterfactual regret minimization connects local regret reduction to
approximate equilibrium in two-player zero-sum games with perfect recall \cite{zinkevich2007cfr},
whereas improved head-to-head win rates characterize the particular matchups
measured here. Population training and exploitability estimation are therefore
separate extensions, not consequences of the present PPO result.

\subsection{Search and policy improvement}
Information Set Monte Carlo Tree Search organizes search around information
sets rather than individual hidden states \cite{cowling2012ismcts}.
ReBeL combines reinforcement learning and search using public belief states
in imperfect-information games \cite{brown2020rebel}. These approaches make
information handling part of the algorithm: a simulated hidden world cannot
simply be treated as information available to the acting policy.

Rollout-based approximate policy iteration turns simulated action comparisons
into supervised policy learning \cite{dimitrakakis2008rollout}. Coupling rollouts
with common random numbers can reduce the variance of relative utilities when
the coupling satisfies suitable conditions \cite{yadav2026common}. Our paired
extension uses these established ideas to supervise public expert choices from
complete-match outcomes. It measures the induced covariance instead of assuming
that shared randomness always reduces variance.

Expert Iteration alternates search-based policy improvement with learning a
policy that generalizes its decisions \cite{anthony2017expert}. This provides
another route to improving an existing actor, with search cost paid during
training or decision making. Our retained rollout experiments evaluate a
simpler alternative, using observation-only policies inside sampled worlds.
Their failure to replicate a match-level benefit motivates the search-free
adaptation and expert-composition experiments studied here.

\subsection{Policy composition and empirical evaluation}
Learning when to switch among existing policies predates this study.
Comanici and Precup formulate switching through options and learn termination
conditions by policy gradients \cite{comanici2010switching}. Our proposed
extension selects between expert action proposals at each decision rather
than learning durations of temporally extended options. Its specific question
is whether differences between point-oriented and win-oriented Koi-Koi policies
provide a useful restricted exploration space for match optimization.
The comparison must establish the value of that restriction and of public
context; switching alone is not the claimed innovation.

Reliable reinforcement-learning evaluation also requires separating uncertainty
in sampled tasks or episodes from variation across training runs.
Agarwal et al. document the instability of conclusions based on a few runs
and advocate uncertainty-aware reporting \cite{agarwal2021precipice}.
We report each trained model separately, exchange player seats on the same
deals, and bootstrap whole deal pairs. Development comparisons and held-out
confirmation use distinct deals. The resulting intervals quantify deal
variation for each fixed model; two training seeds provide limited evidence
about variation across future training runs.

\section{Rules, observations, and comparator}
\subsection{Versioned game protocols}
The primary protocol, \texttt{paper8}, follows the pinned public implementation:
at most eight rounds, 30 initial points per player, and termination when either
player's points reach zero or below. The separate \texttt{research12} protocol
uses twelve rounds and disables bankruptcy termination. Other round rules
are shared. Results from these protocols are not interchangeable.

A round deals eight cards to each hand, eight to the field, and leaves 24 in
the stock. A player discards, resolves a matching capture, reveals a stock
card, resolves that capture, and may be offered stop/continue after an
increase in scored combinations. Forced transitions are folded into the
simulator step. In the pinned scoring convention there is no seven-point
doubling or opponent-koi doubling. After an exhausted round without a scoring
stop, the initial dealer receives one point. The repository's versioned
\texttt{docs/RULES.md} supplies the complete scoring contract, including
nonstandard combinations inherited from the comparator.

The action space has 48 card actions and two stop/continue actions. Decision
phases are discard, capture from the hand play, capture from the stock reveal,
and koi. The two capture phases share an actor. Policies receive their own
hand and public state/history; the opponent's hand, stock order, random-number
state, and future events are excluded. A legal-action mask is applied before
sampling or greedy selection.

\subsection{Pinned opponents and public assistance}
We pin the released source and load the original RL-WP and RL-Point
weights without further training. The reproducibility manifest identifies
the revision and all six weight files. The comparison uses the public
endgame guard corresponding to upstream assistance for both sides. This guard
restricts actions when a public match-outcome decision is solved, including
stopping with a winning final-round score. It does not inspect hidden cards.

The primary opponent is assisted RL-WP. Assisted RL-Point is a distinct
secondary opponent, because point-oriented and win-oriented training need not
produce the same ordering. All final match evaluations use strict CPU
inference and greedy actions. GPU feature/inference checks support training
compatibility but do not establish universal floating-point action identity.

\section{Self-play adaptation}
\subsection{Policy and value functions}
We initialize the three phase networks from the original RL-WP weights.
Their inherited raw action scores $q_\theta(o,a)$ are not probabilities.
For the publicly legal action set $\mathcal A(o)$, training samples from
\begin{equation}
 \pi_{\theta,T}(a\mid o)=
 \frac{\exp(q_\theta(o,a)/T)}
 {\sum_{b\in\mathcal A(o)}\exp(q_\theta(o,b)/T)},\qquad a\in\mathcal A(o).
\end{equation}
The public endgame guard can further narrow $\mathcal A(o)$. At evaluation,
the agent chooses a maximizing legal score, so a positive temperature does not
change the mathematical argmax for fixed scores. Temperature can nevertheless
change the learned policy through sampling and optimization. Dropout remains
disabled during both collection and gradient updates, preserving a consistent
policy for stored log probabilities.

The inherited actors retain the upstream 256-wide, two-layer Transformer
architecture. Native features reproduce the original 300-by-50 representation,
including its historical input conventions. A separate value network receives
a 560-dimensional public/own observation and uses two 256-unit tanh hidden
layers followed by a scalar output. Its output layer is initialized to zero;
it shares no actor parameters. Both actor and critic use only information available to the acting player.

\subsection{Player-relative returns}
Both players use the same current stochastic policy during self-play. Rewards
are zero until match termination, when the acting player receives $+1$, $0$,
or $-1$ for a win, draw, or loss. We use an undiscounted, player-relative
adaptation of generalized advantage estimation \cite{schulman2015gae}.
Let $d_t$ indicate match termination and let $s_t$ be $+1$ when consecutive
decision states have the same acting player and $-1$ otherwise. Then
\begin{align}
 \delta_t &= r_t+(1-d_t)s_t V(o_{t+1})-V(o_t),\\
 A_t &= \delta_t+(1-d_t)s_t\lambda A_{t+1},\qquad \lambda=0.95.
\end{align}
Rollout truncation bootstraps from the next observation. Round boundaries
retain continuation value unless the match ends. A terminal transition masks
the value of the automatically reset next match. The sign follows the actual
next acting player, which need not alternate at every decision phase.

Advantages are normalized over each rollout batch before optimization.
We optimize the clipped PPO surrogate with clipping parameter 0.2, add
one-half the value mean-squared error, and subtract 0.02 times policy entropy.
The global trainable gradient norm is clipped at 0.5. Adam uses
$\epsilon=10^{-5}$ and separate actor/value learning-rate groups. The first
20 updates freeze actor parameters and fit only the critic; the next 500
updates train both. This warmup is part of the reported budget.

\subsection{Fixed development comparison}
Each update collects 128 decisions from each of 128 parallel environments and
uses three minibatch epochs with minibatch size 512. Each model therefore
receives exactly 8,519,680 simulator decisions in 520 updates. The value
learning rate is $10^{-4}$. The warm setting uses $T=0.05$ and actor learning
rate $3\times10^{-6}$; the cold setting uses $T=0.01$ and
$6\times10^{-7}$. Both settings are trained at seeds 6710001 and 6720001.
Only the final update-520 checkpoints enter the comparison. Intermediate
snapshots are retained for accounting and recovery, not strength selection.

This is a comparison of two parameter bundles, not an isolated temperature
ablation. Although the two settings have the same learning-rate/temperature
ratio, they can differ in sampled trajectories, entropy, local gradients, and
adaptive optimizer states. We do not attribute a difference solely to one
mechanism. Execution is eager float32 with TF32 disabled. The GPU is an
NVIDIA GeForce RTX 5070 Ti; the runtime records PyTorch 2.12.0+cu130 and NumPy
2.5.4. Evaluation is kept separate from training.

\section{Evaluation and selection}
\subsection{Duplicate deals and uncertainty}
For each seed we play two matches with candidate/opponent seats exchanged,
retaining the same seat-assigned deal streams and initial dealer. If the
candidate's two match scores are $w_{i0},w_{i1}\in\{0,1/2,1\}$, the observation
for inference is $x_i=(w_{i0}+w_{i1})/2$. The reported draw-adjusted win score
is $\bar x$. We resample whole pairs, not individual match legs, for 5,000
bootstrap replicates using random seed 99 and linearly interpolated 2.5th and
97.5th percentiles. Paired method comparisons resample the per-deal differences.
These intervals describe deal variation conditional on each fixed model;
they do not measure uncertainty over an arbitrary distribution of training
seeds or guarantee exact finite-sample coverage.

Point difference is final candidate points minus opponent points, averaged
across the two seats. Because total points remain 60, this is twice the
candidate's final-points-minus-initial-30 statistic used in the original paper.
Positive expected points are reported separately and cannot substitute for a
win-score criterion. Shared deals across models or opponents do not create
additional independent deal samples.

\subsection{Development and frozen final rules}
The development study uses 5,000 duplicate pairs with seeds
7,200,000--7,204,999 for the initial policy and four trained endpoints. An arm
qualifies only if both training seeds have an absolute win-score 95\% interval
lower bound above 0.5. A cold-over-warm benefit requires positive lower bounds
for both paired score differences. Selection chooses cold if it qualifies and
shows that replicated benefit; otherwise it chooses qualified warm, then
qualified cold, and otherwise no method. The canonical model always uses
training seed 6710001 of the selected arm. There is no best-seed ranking.

These rules were fixed before this study's outcomes, but the research program
had already explored other methods and development deals. Consequently,
development qualification is not untouched confirmation. In particular, an
earlier two-seed screen of the warm method at 1,000 pairs was inconclusive.
We retain that result and use fresh model seeds in the present comparison.

The frozen final test evaluates both selected models against assisted RL-WP
and, separately, assisted RL-Point. Each of four jobs plays 10,000 duplicate
pairs on seeds 100,000,000--100,009,999: 80,000 matches on 10,000 unique deal
streams. The primary criterion requires both RL-WP interval lower bounds to
exceed 0.5. The secondary RL-Point criterion is recorded separately. There is
no model reranking, training, extension, or early strength stopping after
accessing these final outcomes. Four two-thread CPU workers run concurrently;
their recorded times are not an isolated latency benchmark.

\section{Systems, budgets, and negative evidence}

Python reference rules and shared C++/CUDA transitions implement the same
versioned profiles. Differential tests exercise CPU/GPU transitions, public
observations, legal masks, and player-relative signs. Native public features
are checked against the pinned model inputs. A recorded compatibility corpus
contains 12,861 decisions from 100 complete random matches, 40 cached decision
states, and 128 hidden-state variants, totaling 13,029 active states. On this
finite corpus native CPU/CUDA features agree with the original inputs and
both original policies have zero observed assisted-action disagreements at
the tested batch shape. CPU inference is used for the reported strength comparisons.

Checkpoints retain model weights, optimizer state, environment state, random
states, configuration, counters, and source identities. Resume checks cover
the exact next update on CPU and CUDA. Independent experiment auditors
recompute artifact digests, optimizer-step accounting, raw pair statistics,

\subsection{Retained unsuccessful directions}
The broader development record includes architecture/critic variants,
koi-focused adaptation, direct published-opponent training, full-match return
experiments, and search. The repository's \texttt{docs/EXPERIMENTS.md} is the
chronological ledger; it contains pilot sizes, negative results, and subsequent
diagnostics. These studies explain the choices leading to the present method.

One fixed full-action rollout search extension improved the first tested model
but did not replicate on a second: its latter absolute score was 48.000\%
[46.225, 49.825], with a paired gain of 0.175 percentage points
[-0.750, 1.150] over no search. Independent confirmation samples for proposed
action changes also failed the both-model win-gain criterion. Separate
diagnostics found that selected-world gains shrink on independent samples and
that opponent-hand support assumptions can materially alter values at chosen
roots. Those selected-state diagnostics do not establish match-level benefits.
The final selected policy consequently uses no search.

\section{Public expert arbitration: extension under evaluation}
\label{sec:arbitration}
The RL-Point comparison suggests that useful decisions may remain in a
point-oriented policy even after match-win adaptation. We implement a
controller over two frozen experts: an adapted RL-WP parent and the original
RL-Point agent. For public observation $o$, let their legal proposals be
$a_W(o)$ and $a_P(o)$, after the same public endgame guard. The controller
acts on the distinct proposal set
\begin{equation}
 \mathcal C(o)=\{a_W(o),a_P(o)\},\qquad
 \mu_\phi(a\mid o)=\operatorname{softmax}_{a\in\mathcal C(o)}g_\phi(o,a).
\end{equation}
If both experts agree, $\mathcal C(o)$ contains one action and the policy
log-probability has zero gradient. Exploration is concentrated on disagreements.
This construction preserves agreement decisions and limits trainable choices
to actions already favored by one of the two experts.

The full controller receives the 560 public features and one-hot encodings
of the two proposals, followed by two 128-unit tanh layers. Its initial
probability of the parent proposal is 0.9 at disagreements, so initial greedy
play selects the frozen parent expert proposal. Both experts remain frozen; only the controller
and a public critic are trained with terminal-match PPO. The critic starts
from the parent's value network.

The ongoing fixed-budget study compares this controller with a phase-only
linear selector and ordinary continued Transformer PPO. Each of two parent
models initializes all three arms. Every arm receives 20 critic-only and 500
policy updates, or 8,519,680 additional simulator decisions. Only final
endpoints are evaluated, on 1,000 new duplicate deals against RL-WP, RL-Point,
and the corresponding parent. Both unchanged parents also play the published
opponents on these deals. Full versus phase changes capacity as well as
information; additional ablations are needed to isolate particular context
features. The full protocol and implementation are available in
\path{docs/ARBITRATION.md}. No performance benefit is attributed to this
extension before those comparisons finish.


\subsection{Paired complete-match supervision}
A second implementation replaces PPO credit assignment with direct comparisons
at expert disagreements. Under a fixed behavior mixture (90\% parent, 10\%
Point), the simulator copies the current world into two branches. Each branch
executes one expert proposal; both players then follow the frozen parent to
match termination. Continuation policies see only their own observations.
The branches share hidden cards and future-round deal streams. For root-player
returns $G_W,G_P\in\{-1,0,1\}$, the regression target is
\begin{equation}
  \delta=G_P-G_W, \qquad
  \mathcal{L}(\phi)=\mathbb{E}[(h_\phi(o,a_W,a_P)-\delta)^2].
\end{equation}
The controller selects the proposal with the higher predicted return, with
native action-index tie breaking. Conditional on its public input, the target
averages the difference over hidden worlds encountered by the behavior policy.
Training exports public inputs and outcomes; simulator state stays outside the
learned controller.

The fixed study collects 65,536 disagreement roots per parent and fits the
controller for 20 epochs. A matched ablation removes explicit round and score
inputs while preserving architecture, data and initialization. Entire source
matches are held out for validation. Diagnostics report prediction error and
the observed one-action gain on these held-out roots, with uncertainty clustered
by source match. Repeated deployment is evaluated separately in full matches.
Generation decisions, branch completions and fitting work are counted separately;
this method incurs rollout computation beyond the PPO training budget.

Both fitted controllers, arbitration PPO, continued actor PPO and unchanged
parents are evaluated on 1,000 duplicate development deals against RL-WP and
RL-Point. Two fixed hybrids assign card play to one expert and koi-koi decisions
to the other. These controls test whether learning adds value beyond simple
role substitution. The protocol and implementation are recorded in
\texttt{docs/PAIRED\_ROLLOUTS.md}; comparative results are pending.

\section{Limitations and interpretation}
The strongest supported result is the improvement in match wins against
assisted RL-WP at both adapted model seeds. RL-Point remains competitive in
wins and superior in expected points. This objective dependence is a useful
direction for further work: more aggressive scoring may be valuable when
trailing, while a leading player can favor decisions that preserve a match win.
The arbitration study tests whether learned public-state decisions can use
this difference.

Two training seeds limit conclusions about robustness across optimization
runs. The broader development program also includes many unsuccessful
interventions, which are retained in the experiment ledger. Held-out deals
support the reported endpoint comparisons, while additional seeds and opponents
are needed to assess generality. Exploitability and human performance remain
unmeasured. The temperature comparison changes learning rate simultaneously,
and latency describes one machine and a cached random-play workload.

\section{Conclusion}
Self-play adaptation improves the match-winning performance of published
Koi-Koi Transformers. Both adapted models beat assisted RL-WP on held-out
deals after 8.52 million additional decisions each, with search-free CPU
inference averaging 1.585 ms for the canonical model. RL-Point exposes the
remaining challenge: match-win improvement is inconsistent across models,
and expected points still favor the point-oriented agent. The public expert
arbitration experiment tests whether those different strengths can be combined
through learned decisions at expert disagreements.

and development ranges in \path{runs/arbitration_study/protocol.json}.
\path{docs/ARBITRATION.md} describes the arms, budgets and evaluation criteria.

The new arbitration experiments freeze the inherited actors. A traced
near-tie shows why this detail belongs to the inference specification: with
identical weights and public inputs, changing only parameter requires-grad
flags changes a stop/continue score by about $10^{-6}$ and breaks a tie.
The first parent's phase-only controller always selects its frozen parent
proposal, yet one of 1,000 Point pairs differs from the older deployed wrapper
in points; its aggregate win score is unchanged and mean points differ by 0.002.
Both learned controller arms share the frozen expert path. The diagnostic in
\path{docs/ARBITRATION.md} records the state and reproducible flag intervention.

The isolated latency benchmark times the deployment call, including features
and public assistance, on one Ryzen 9 9900X with two PyTorch threads. It uses
1,287 unique cached states with overlapping phase and mixed groups, two warmup
passes and eight measured passes. The reported quantiles summarize repeated
calls. Loading, simulator transitions and correctness checks lie outside the
timed region. Raw timings and corpus identities are included in the ledger.

Build instructions are in \path{docs/paper/README.rst};
\path{docs/ARTIFACTS.rst} describes the existing local archive, permitted
comparator files and verification commands. The archive preserves the completed
study rather than the ongoing extension. The broader exploratory record,
including unsuccessful experiments, is retained in \path{docs/EXPERIMENTS.md}.
