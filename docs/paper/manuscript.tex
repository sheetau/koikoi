\begin{abstract}
A computation limit can prevent an imperfect-information game controller
from finishing its baseline evaluation, even when some future decisions admit
provable improvements. We show how to recover those improvements from public
play in Koi-Koi. The controller identifies a fixed opponent from revealed
hands, constructs a complete partition of future own/public histories, and
adopts executable continuations whose lower value bounds exceed the baseline's
upper bounds. Their arrival-weighted gains certify improvement without a
completed root reference. Public outcome certificates compress the programs,
and all local attempts share one transition allowance. This makes unfinished
global evaluation compatible with verified local policy changes. On 8,192
independent sources, the base controller improves expected terminal win score
for both parents against both fixed opponents; mean equal-opponent gains are
0.2819 and 0.2143 percentage points. A separate 4,096-source study verifies an
additional frontier gain for both parents, averaging 0.00128 and 0.000784
percentage points. The extension improves 26 source beliefs; realized scores
are unchanged and actual search work increases. The result establishes a
way to retain useful policy improvements under computation limits, with
coverage and efficiency determining its practical impact.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
A strong game-playing policy can still make locally improvable decisions.
Search can correct them, but in an imperfect-information game a proposed
correction must work across all hidden states consistent with the player's
observations. Evaluating even the unchanged policy can exhaust the available
computation. Requiring that evaluation to finish at the current root then
rejects every proposed change, including improvements that could be verified
at smaller future beliefs.

We address this bottleneck by separating the continuations that change from
those that remain identical. The controller follows its parent policy to a
future decision boundary, constructs every arrival belief, and verifies a
complete local program only where it proposes a replacement. A candidate is
adopted when its lower value bound exceeds the parent's upper bound. The
arrival-weighted local gains establish improvement at the root; unchanged
continuations cancel without being valued to termination. The resulting
policy can therefore improve even when the root reference is unresolved.

Koi-Koi provides a concrete test of this construction. Its stop/continue
decisions trade an immediate score against future scoring opportunities, and
public hand discards can eventually reveal an opponent's earlier inputs.
We use that evidence to identify a fixed opponent, then reconstruct current
hidden-card beliefs from the learner's own observations and public play.
The parents are two match-trained adaptations of Guan et al.'s released
Transformer policies \cite{guan2023koikoi,pinnedkoikoi}. They supply strong
baselines while keeping the verified search improvement separately measurable.

The contribution combines a bounded, complete next-discard partition with
executable history-conditioned programs and public outcome certificates.
Independent terminal replay verifies the resulting gains. Fixed population
studies then measure their frequency and size, including sources where search
cannot make a change.

The base controller improves expected win score in all four fixed
parent--opponent populations on 8,192 sources. Its mean mixture gains are
0.2819 and 0.2143 percentage points; the joint 0.1-point lower-bound target
is unmet. On 4,096 separate sources, the frontier extension recovers 26
improvements over that base, with positive simultaneous mixture lower bounds
for both parents. Its additional means, 0.00128 and 0.000784 percentage points,
are small, and realized scores are unchanged. These results establish the
capability to recover otherwise lost improvements. They also identify the
practical research target: cover more useful decisions at lower verification
cost. The same construction can apply to other finite imperfect-information
games when their conditional beliefs and local policy bounds can be certified.

\section{Related work}
\paragraph{Koi-Koi and information from play.}
Guan et al.'s RL-WP and RL-Point Transformers optimize distinct win and point
objectives \cite{guan2023koikoi}. We retain their released representations and
adapt the actors with match-end PPO; those training experiments are supporting
evidence (Appendix~\ref{app:parent-adaptation}). Public revelation and opponent
adaptation have direct precedents in Bayes' Bluff and Bayesian modeling with
partial revelation \cite{southey2005bayes,ganzfried2024opponent}. Policy Based
Inference weights possible hidden deals by action likelihoods in Skat
\cite{rebstock2019policy}. Here, completed discards reconstruct historical inputs
exactly and eliminate inconsistent members of a fixed deterministic model set.
Suzuki's Open Hanafuda AI also combines observable-information inference and
simulation, under different rules \cite{suzuki2026openhanafuda}.

\paragraph{Belief search and verified improvement.}
ISMCTS organizes search around information sets \cite{cowling2012ismcts};
ReBeL combines learning and search in public belief states
\cite{brown2020rebel}; Expert Iteration learns from search-improved decisions
\cite{anthony2017expert}. Bounds already support anytime POMDP planning in
HSVI \cite{smith2004hsvi}. SPIBB, MCTS-SPIBB and its POMDP extension use
baseline constraints to control improvement from finite offline data
\cite{laroche2019spibb,castellini2023mcts,simao2023pomdpspi}. Their uncertainty
comes from learning an environment from data. Our conditional comparison
instead uses a specified opponent and chance law, with exact enumeration or
conservative bounds for computation left unfinished.

Parallel rollout combines heuristic policies in partially observable control
\cite{chang2004parallel}. Policy improvement through future conditional
comparisons is therefore established. Our contribution is the concrete
combination of a bounded, complete public-history partition, executable local
programs and shared work accounting that recovers gains when the parent root
value is unavailable. Burch et al.'s safe subgame decomposition preserves
full-game guarantees using opponent counterfactual values
\cite{burch2014decomposition}. It targets exploitability. Our guarantee targets
expected performance against a fixed opponent under its conditional deal law;
transferring it to equilibrium-oriented play would require a different test.

\paragraph{Measuring small gains.}
Following the need for explicit uncertainty in reinforcement-learning
comparisons \cite{agarwal2021precipice}, we keep fixed policies, development
sources and confirmation sources separate. AIVAT reduces performance-estimate
variance through known strategies and heuristic values \cite{burch2018aivat}.
Our search evaluation instead integrates the changed continuation exactly
and averages its conditional gain over independent source matches. It requires
the source and posterior laws to agree. The supporting adaptation and policy
selection studies have additional precedents in
Appendix~\ref{app:supporting-related}.

\section{Rules, observations, and comparator}
\subsection{Versioned game protocols}
The primary protocol, \texttt{paper8}, follows the pinned public implementation:
at most eight rounds, 30 initial points per player, and termination when either
player's points reach zero or below. The separate \texttt{research12} protocol
uses twelve rounds and disables bankruptcy termination. Other round rules
are shared. Results from these protocols are not interchangeable.

A round deals eight cards to each hand, eight to the field, and leaves 24 in
the stock. A player discards, resolves a matching capture, reveals a stock
card, resolves that capture, and may be offered stop/continue after an
increase in scored combinations. Forced transitions are folded into the
simulator step. In the pinned scoring convention there is no seven-point
doubling or opponent-koi doubling. After an exhausted round without a scoring
stop, the initial dealer receives one point. The repository's versioned
\texttt{docs/RULES.md} supplies the complete scoring contract, including
nonstandard combinations inherited from the comparator.

The action space has 48 card actions and two stop/continue actions. Decision
phases are discard, capture from the hand play, capture from the stock reveal,
and koi. The two capture phases share an actor. Policies receive their own
hand and public state/history; the opponent's hand, stock order, random-number
state, and future events are excluded. A legal-action mask is applied before
sampling or greedy selection.

\subsection{Pinned opponents and public assistance}
We pin the released source and load the original RL-WP and RL-Point
weights without further training. The reproducibility manifest identifies
the revision and all six weight files. The comparison uses the public
endgame guard corresponding to upstream assistance for both sides. This guard
restricts actions when a public match-outcome decision is solved, including
stopping with a winning final-round score. It does not inspect hidden cards.

The primary opponent is assisted RL-WP. Assisted RL-Point is a distinct
secondary opponent, because point-oriented and win-oriented training need not
produce the same ordering. The original paper8 final match evaluations use
strict CPU inference and greedy actions. GPU feature/inference checks support
training compatibility but do not establish universal floating-point action
identity. The separate rule-transfer study uses fixed GPU arithmetic and its
own matched control; its results are not pooled with the original matches.

\subsection{Parents and public opponent evidence}
Two fixed parents are obtained by adapting the released WP actor with
terminal-match PPO. Each receives 20 critic-only and 500 policy updates.
Both beat assisted RL-WP in held-out duplicate play, with win scores of
51.480\% and 51.445\%; the replicated RL-Point criterion fails.
Appendix~\ref{app:parent-adaptation} gives training, selection and all match
results. The search studies compare each parent with its own extension.

The opponent is identified before search activation. Stock draws never enter
a player's hand, so observing all eight hand discards reveals that round's
initial hand. Together with public events, this reconstructs the opponent's
earlier network inputs. We retain WP and Point as fixed deterministic
candidates and eliminate any model that disagrees with an observed action.
Only subsequent decisions may use a unique surviving model. Unresolved or
empty candidate sets do not activate the controller. This procedure assumes
the opponent belongs to the candidate class; its true label is never an input.
Appendix~\ref{sec:public-information} reports the reconstruction checks and
separate learning tests.

\section{Discussion: value, efficiency, and transfer}
\label{sec:discussion}
\subsection{What becomes possible}
The construction changes what a limited search budget can deliver. Failure to
finish the parent root value no longer forces rejection of every policy change.
Search can concentrate verification on the continuations it actually replaces,
while retaining the parent elsewhere. Complete arrival beliefs and commitment
to executable programs make this selective verification valid. This is useful
when a strong existing policy is expensive to evaluate and only a small part
of its future play needs correction.

The population studies establish positive expected gains under fixed laws.
They also put their scale in perspective: the base's joint 0.1-point target
fails, and the extension changes no realized score in its cohort. Two parent
seeds and two fixed opponents do not establish general strength, exploitability
or human performance. The main result is a verified improvement capability;
making it frequent and cheap enough for routine play remains an empirical task.

\subsection{Increasing the frequency and size of improvements}
The incremental study has 1,943 eligible sources out of 4,096. Only 60 reach
the unresolved-parent case addressed by the extension, and 26 of those admit
a gain. Thus the method succeeds in 43.3\% of its targeted cases but changes
only 0.63\% of all source beliefs. This concentrates the opportunity in two
places: reaching more useful comparison sites and increasing the conditional
gain at each site. Raising success among already targeted roots alone cannot
remove the small population coverage.

Earlier activation alone failed: the original eight six-draw probes adopted
no program. Changing the candidate representation makes two of those same
fixtures improvable (Appendix~\ref{app:resumable-proposals}). Each candidate
changes one public action and follows the parent thereafter, so it is complete
before evaluation starts. Persistent interval evaluators then spend the budget
on comparison rather than repeatedly constructing executable continuations.
This is a concrete way to increase feasible coverage, although the reused
fixtures do not establish a population effect. A separate fresh-source study
removes the parent-refusal filter at a common five-draw gate; it measures
direct frontier controllers, not an increment over the original integrated AI.

\subsection{Reducing actual computation}
The unchanged maximum allowance does not mean unchanged cost. The extension
adds 111,874,298 planning transitions, about 27,313 per source match.
Proposal search accounts for 82.7\% of this work; complete-prefix construction
accounts for 0.33\%. Proposal reuse and allocation are therefore higher-priority
targets than optimizing the prefix alone. Actor evaluations are already cached by public observation. A new
three-arm development comparison isolates completed-subprogram reuse from
budget allocation (Appendix~\ref{app:search-reuse}). Reuse saves 3.03\% of
physical transitions within the same refinement schedule, but that schedule
retains only 5.56\% of the existing allocation's summed improvement. Thus
cheaper repeated computation does not by itself produce better candidates.
Parent-default proposals address that construction cost directly. On the same
eight five-draw fixtures, their exact gain rises by 32.40\% for 7.17\% more
transitions: 23.55\% more gain per transition. Positive-source coverage remains
4/8, and elapsed time does not improve. The result supports a better
finite-budget candidate representation, not a general search speedup.

Public outcome certificates already reduce transition counts in development
replay. Extending their coverage could both save work and make more programs
complete under the same cap. Wall-clock improvement still needs a controlled
measurement: native transitions omit neural-inference, bookkeeping and memory
costs. A useful efficiency study should compare fixed-budget gain and the
time needed to reach a fixed gain, while checking that optimized inference
preserves the actions on which exact verification depends.

\subsection{Transfer to other imperfect-information games}
The transferable component is the partition-and-compare construction in
Equation~\ref{eq:frontier-gain}. It requires a specified conditional opponent
and chance model, a complete first-arrival partition, executable replacements,
and valid candidate-lower/parent-upper comparisons. Neither hand revelation
nor the Koi-Koi discard boundary is required by that argument. Small finite
poker variants and short trick-taking endgames are concrete candidates for
testing it. Policy-based hidden-deal inference in Skat provides a precedent
for constructing beliefs from public actions \cite{rebstock2019policy}.

The game-specific work is substantial. A new domain needs a tractable boundary
and its own bounds or outcome certificates; Koi-Koi's prefix bound does not
carry over. Stochastic opponents require action-likelihood weighting instead
of deterministic elimination. Unknown or adapting opponents require model
uncertainty to enter the adoption bounds before the same guarantee can be
claimed. Safe equilibrium-oriented re-solving addresses a different target
\cite{burch2014decomposition,brown2020rebel}. A convincing transfer experiment
would first use a small game with full terminal verification, then compare
complete-root and frontier controllers at matched budgets, reporting eligible
mass, adoption frequency, expected gain, realized scores and elapsed time.

\section{Conclusion}
Verified local policy changes can survive an unfinished global evaluation.
In Koi-Koi, public opponent evidence, complete arrival beliefs and executable
continuations make this capability concrete under a shared computation limit.
The frontier construction recovers improvements that the base controller
rejects because its parent reference is unresolved. Independent population
evaluation establishes a positive expected increment for both adapted parents.

The increment is small and requires additional work. Its value is the verified
recovery mechanism and the clear route it gives for further improvement:
reach more useful beliefs, complete more local programs and reduce their
verification cost. Testing those mechanisms in another finite
imperfect-information game is the next step toward demonstrating broader
practical value.

\section{Observer-bootstrap calculation and learner-decision returns}
\label{app:observer}
\subsection{Exact terminal-game calculation}
At the uniform policy, $v_x=(\mu_{x0}+\mu_{x1})/2$ and
$m_a=(\mu_{0a}+\mu_{1a})/2$. The learner's immediate reward is zero and the
opponent's terminal reward is $-R$. The first residual is $m_A-v_X$; the second
is $m_A-R$. Applying the negative player-transition sign yields
$(m_A-v_X)-\lambda(m_A-R)$, proving
Equation~\ref{eq:observer-advantage}.

First parameterize the two logits independently as $\theta_0,\theta_1$.
Critic targets are detached in the policy update. The score for parameter $\theta_x$ is
$\mathbf1\{X=x\}(A-1/2)$. Since $X$ is uniform,
\begin{equation}
 g_x(\lambda)=\frac{\lambda(\mu_{x1}-\mu_{x0})
 +(1-\lambda)(m_1-m_0)}{8}.
\end{equation}
The true gradient is $(1/800,-1/4)$, with first component $0.00125$.
Signed GAE at $\lambda=0.95$ gives $g_0=-161/32000=-0.00503125$ and
$g_1=-0.24371875$. The first component is positive exactly
when $\lambda>199/201$. These are exact expectations over the finite game,
not estimated confidence intervals. The single-transition and $\lambda=1$
controls use $R-v_X$; conditioning the intermediate critic on $(X,A)$ also
restores the expectation. The first-component sample variance is about
0.1197453 for the single transition versus 0.1080973 for signed GAE at $\lambda=0.95$.
The correction therefore changes variance as well as bias.

Now restrict the logits to $(\theta,-\alpha\theta)$ for fixed $\alpha$.
The scalar score is $(A-1/2)c_X$ with $c=(1,-\alpha)$. The chain rule gives
\begin{align}
 J'(0)&=\frac{1+200\alpha}{800},\\
 g_{\rm shared}(\lambda)&=g_0(\lambda)-\alpha g_1(\lambda)
 =\frac{201(1+\alpha)\lambda-199(1-\alpha)}{1600}.
\end{align}
For $\alpha=1/100$ and $\lambda=19/20$, these are $3/800$ and
$-8301/3200000$. The true objective's directional derivative along the expected
update is their product:
\begin{equation}
 J'(0)\,g_{\rm shared}(19/20)=-\frac{24903}{2560000000}<0.
\end{equation}
The positive-sign threshold
is $\lambda>6567/6767$. All three controls recover $3/800$. This establishes
local descent in the stated shared-parameter family; the independently
parameterized policy above has only one reversed coordinate.

The executable diagnostic is \path{scripts/diagnose_observer_bootstrap.py}.
It enumerates all signal/action/payoff combinations, including zero-probability
outcomes, checks the conditional critic means and compares analytic gradients
with the return implementations to a gradient tolerance of $3\times10^{-8}$.
The evidence exporter verifies both retained
CPU/CUDA reports, their source identities and the rational calculation. No
Koi-Koi matches or policy updates contribute to this example.
The shared-parameter extension, \path{scripts/diagnose_shared_observer_bootstrap.py},
uses rational outcome enumeration and direct scalar autograd. It reruns the
Python/native CPU calculation and projects the pinned earlier CPU/CUDA gradients
by $(1,-1/100)$; it performs no additional CUDA computation. The evidence ledger
binds both calculations and their exact source files.

\subsection{Internal and boundary bootstrap terms}
\label{app:trace}
The standard GAE expansion \cite{schulman2015gae} identifies the effect of
an observer change at any rollout position. Fix a root action by seat $i$,
use $\gamma=1$, and express rewards $r_k$ and values in that seat's payoff
units. If $p_k$ is the active seat, write
$W_k=(-1)^{i+p_k}V_k^{p_k}$ and let $G_k$ be the realized return through match
termination, including any suffix beyond the $n$-step rollout. Thus
$G_k=r_k+G_{k+1}$. Consecutive player-change signs telescope to $(-1)^{i+p_k}$,
including repeated actions by the same player. With $e_k=W_k-G_k$,
\begin{align}
 \widehat A_\lambda
 &=\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\lambda^k(r_k+W_{k+1}-W_k)\\
 &=G_0-W_0+(1-\lambda)\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}\lambda^{k-1}e_k
   +\lambda^{n-1}e_n.\label{eq:trace-error}
\end{align}
This pathwise identity follows by substituting $r_k=G_k-G_{k+1}$ and
collecting coefficients; $0^0=1$. A terminal boundary has $W_n=G_n=0$.
A nonterminal cutoff generally retains $e_n$, including when $\lambda=1$.

Let $S_0=\nabla\log\pi_i(a_0\mid H_0^i)$, hold the opponent policy fixed,
and treat targets as constants. If $W_0$ is action-independent given the
root policy information, multiplying the last two terms of
Equation~\ref{eq:trace-error} by $S_0$ and taking expectations gives the
error in this root score contribution. Exactness conditional on the active
opponent's observation need not make $\mathbb E[S_0e_k]$ zero. In contrast,
let $U_k=\mathbb E[G_k\mid H_k^i]$ use the root player's full history,
including its root action. When $S_0$ is measurable in $H_k^i$,
\begin{equation}
 \mathbb E[S_0(U_k-G_k)]
 =\mathbb E[S_0\,\mathbb E[U_k-G_k\mid H_k^i]]=0.
\end{equation}
The same argument applies at a nonterminal cutoff and requires no opponent
hand. Finite feature vectors need not retain this history. The calculation
concerns one player's score contribution; updating both seats of a shared
zero-sum policy does not turn their identically zero total payoff into a
joint improvement objective.

For the scalar example, cut the rollout immediately after the learner action.
Exact enumeration gives
\begin{align}
 \mathbb E[S_0(m_A-\mu_{XA})]&=\TraceScoreCovariance,\\
 g_{\rm cutoff}(\lambda)&=\TraceSignedCutoff,\qquad 0\leq\lambda\leq1.
\end{align}
The true gradient and the exact learner-history update are both $3/800$.
The diagnostic
\path{scripts/diagnose_observer_trace_identity.py} verifies the pathwise
identity in \TraceExactCases{} rational cases and all 16 combinations of
terminal/cutoff, return construction and tested $\lambda$ values. CPU returns
agree within $1.513\times10^{-9}$. This establishes the cutoff failure in the
example; attributing a Koi-Koi performance difference to that mechanism
requires a separate controlled comparison.

\subsection{Learner-decision implementation}

The companion \path{scripts/learner_returns.py} accumulates fixed-opponent
transitions from a learner action to its next observation or match termination.
It uses learner-relative rewards, including opponent-caused terminal rewards,
and $\lambda^k$ eligibility decay across $k$ primitive decisions. At a rollout
boundary, a final learner action without a next learner observation or terminal
outcome is excluded from both policy and value losses. Its starting value still
bootstraps the preceding completed transition. Reset masks block rewards from
the next match. Simulator work, learner actions, valid loss rows and omitted
tails are counted separately.

The integrated PPO variant also removes the opponent critic and normalizes
value loss over valid learner rows, changing joint clipping and minibatch sizes.
These are part of the full intervention. The fixed comparison in
\path{docs/LEARNER_RETURN_STUDY.rst} includes the original signed estimator and
$\lambda=1$ controls; Appendix~\ref{sec:learner} reports the completed results.
Finite current observations and boundary-dependent exclusions remain part of
the implemented estimator. The exact example concerns its stated finite game;
the Koi-Koi comparisons test the full learning procedures, including their
optimization differences.

\section{Learning against fixed opponents}
\label{sec:training}
We test two changes to the adapted actors: training directly against the
published opponents, and constructing returns between learner decisions.
The first comparison measures opponent-specific training against continued
self-play. The second holds the opponent mixture fixed and changes the return
construction and its associated optimization.

\subsection{Direct training against the published opponents}
\label{sec:response}
The first comparison tests full-policy PPO against the published opponents.
Each retained parent initializes two continuations: a response learner facing
RL-WP and RL-Point on a balanced whole-match schedule, and ordinary self-play.
Both use the warm configuration and 8,519,680 additional simulator decisions,
including 20 critic-only and 500 policy updates. The response actor learns only
from its own actions, with separate observation critics for the two acting roles.
The budget counts both players' actions; equal simulator work therefore gives
different learner sample counts across the two regimes.

The four final endpoints and two unchanged parents each face WP and Point on
5,000 fresh shared duplicate deals, giving 120,000 matches. The primary criterion
requires a positive lower 95\% paired win-change bound for response versus both
its parent and its self-play control, at both initial models against both
opponents. No comparison passes (Table~\ref{tab:response}).

\subsection{Learner-decision returns}
\label{sec:learner}
The exact example in Appendix~\ref{sec:observer} motivates a comparison of three
return constructions: signed GAE with $\lambda=0.95$, learner-decision returns
with $\lambda=0.95$, and signed GAE with $\lambda=1$. The learner-decision variant
bootstraps at learner observations and attributes opponent-caused terminal
rewards to the learner before a match reset. Eligibility decays across primitive
decisions, and unfinished learner-to-opponent tails are excluded from both

\section{Fixed-observer self-play}
\label{sec:observer-selfplay}
\subsection{Returns with a consistent viewer}
The finite-game analysis suggests retaining the same player's information at
each bootstrap. We implement this in self-play by evaluating two separate
critic rows at every primitive decision. Each row contains its viewer's own
cards and public information, plus a bit indicating whether that viewer is
acting. A shared MLP maps the resulting 561 features to $v_t^i$, the value for
viewer $i$. It processes rows independently; neither row contains the other
player's hand or the stock order. The actor and its input are unchanged.

Let $r_t$ denote seat zero's terminal-match reward and $c_t$ indicate that the
match continues after decision $t$. For each viewer $i\in\{0,1\}$, use
\begin{align}
 \delta_t^i &= (-1)^i r_t+c_t v_{t+1}^i-v_t^i,\\
 \widehat A_t^i &= \delta_t^i+\lambda c_t\widehat A_{t+1}^i.
\end{align}
A round ending leaves $c_t=1$; a match ending sets it to zero. At a rollout
cutoff, the recurrence ends with $\widehat A_H^i=0$ and retains each viewer's
own $v_H^i$ bootstrap. Fit $v_t^i+\widehat A_t^i$ for both viewers and use the
acting viewer's advantage for the policy. The two conditional values need not
sum to zero: their observations contain different private information.

The matched signed-return control uses the same two-row critic targets,
architecture, optimizer, minibatches and clipping. It changes only the policy
advantage, selecting active-viewer values for the historical signed recurrence.
Both arms use $\lambda=0.95$ and the warm adaptation settings. For each parent,
they start from the same retained actor and padded critic; all saved model,
optimizer, environment and RNG states agree after the 20
critic-only warmup updates. Each arm then performs 500 policy updates, for
8,519,680 simulator decisions and 17,039,360 value rows in total. This separates
the policy-return contrast from changes in critic fitting.

\section{Expert-controller protocols}
\label{app:controllers}
\subsection{PPO controller and matched continuation}
The controller uses two frozen experts: the adapted RL-WP parent and original
RL-Point. For acting-player observation $o$, let their legal proposals be
$a_W(o)$ and $a_P(o)$, after the same public endgame guard. The controller
acts on the distinct proposal set
\begin{equation}
 \mathcal C(o)=\{a_W(o),a_P(o)\},\qquad
 \mu_\phi(a\mid o)=\operatorname{softmax}_{a\in\mathcal C(o)}g_\phi(o,a).
\end{equation}
At agreement states, $\mathcal C(o)$ has one action and the policy
log-probability has zero gradient. Trainable choices occur only at disagreements.

The full controller receives the 560 observation features and one-hot encodings
of the two proposals, followed by two 128-unit tanh layers. Its initial
probability of the parent proposal is 0.9 at disagreements, so initial greedy
play selects the frozen parent expert proposal. Both experts remain frozen; only the controller
and an observation critic are trained with terminal-match PPO. The critic starts
from the parent's value network.

The fixed-budget study compares this controller with a phase-only
linear selector and ordinary continued Transformer PPO. Each of two parent
models initializes all three arms. Every arm receives 20 critic-only and 500
policy updates, or 8,519,680 additional simulator decisions. Only final
endpoints are evaluated, on 1,000 new duplicate deals against RL-WP, RL-Point,
and the corresponding parent. Both unchanged parents also play the published
opponents on these deals. Full versus phase changes capacity as well as
information; additional ablations are needed to isolate particular context
features. The full protocol and implementation are available in
\path{docs/ARBITRATION.md}.

\subsection{Paired-outcome regression}
A second implementation replaces PPO credit assignment with direct comparisons
at expert disagreements. Under a fixed behavior mixture (90\% parent, 10\%
Point), the simulator copies the current world into two branches. Each branch
executes one expert proposal; both players then follow the frozen parent to
match termination. Continuation policies see only their own observations.
The branches share hidden cards and future-round deal streams. For root-player
returns $G_W,G_P\in\{-1,0,1\}$, the regression target is
\begin{equation}
  \delta=G_P-G_W, \qquad
  \mathcal{L}(\phi)=\mathbb{E}[(h_\phi(o,a_W,a_P)-\delta)^2].
\end{equation}
The controller selects the proposal with the higher predicted return, with
native action-index tie breaking. Conditional on its observation, the target
averages the difference over hidden worlds encountered by the behavior policy.
Training exports observations and outcomes; simulator state stays outside the
learned controller.

The fixed study collects 65,536 disagreement roots per parent and fits the
controller for 20 epochs. A matched ablation removes explicit round and score
inputs while preserving architecture, data and initialization. Entire source
matches are held out for validation. Diagnostics report prediction error and
the observed one-action gain on these held-out roots, with uncertainty clustered
by source match. Repeated deployment is evaluated separately in full matches.
Generation decisions, branch completions and fitting work are counted separately;
this method incurs rollout computation beyond the PPO training budget.

parents against both opponents (Table~\ref{tab:paired-supervision}). The full
first-parent controller gains 1.260 expected points [0.160, 2.430] against WP,
while the second gains 1.660 [0.412, 2.863] against Point. These point gains
occur in different matchups and do not translate into replicated win gains.
Full-versus-ablation win intervals include zero in all four comparisons.

The targets also expose a learning difficulty: only about 9.6\% of roots
have different terminal win outcomes for the two proposals. On held-out
source matches, both full controllers have slightly higher prediction MSE
than predicting zero. Direct access to paired outcomes therefore does not by
itself provide a selector that improves repeated match play. Appendix controls
examine representation, data volume, regularization and continuation opponents.
Protocols are in \texttt{docs/PAIRED\_ROLLOUTS.md}.
