\begin{abstract}
We construct a public-information endgame controller for Koi-Koi that identifies
a fixed WP/Point opponent from completed hands, reconstructs hidden-card
beliefs, and commits to a complete contingent policy. Exact comparisons retain
improvements over a parent actor under a computation cap. Replacing explicit
decisions in guaranteed-win regions with a checked parent continuation reduces
controller planning transitions by 8.84\% on 512 saved source matches, while
increasing adopted improvements from 83 to 86 under unchanged caps. Mean
conditional gains over the two parents are 0.2651 and 0.3290 percentage points;
every realized win score remains unchanged from the preceding controller.
Additional match-win improvement remains unconfirmed. The parent actors are
obtained by terminal-match self-play from the published win policy. After 8.52
million additional decisions each, they achieve 51.480\% and 51.445\%
draw-adjusted win scores against assisted RL-WP on 10,000 held-out duplicate
deals; both 95\% intervals exclude 50\%. They retain the published Transformer
architecture and greedy inference. An exact shared-parameter counterexample
also shows why switching between players' critics can reverse the expected
policy update, motivating returns between learner decisions.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Koi-Koi rewards both collecting valuable card combinations and deciding when
to stop. Continuing can increase the gain, but it also gives the opponent
another chance to score. Across a match, the score and rounds remaining can
change the best decision for the same hand. Expected points and match wins
are therefore distinct learning objectives.

Guan et al. trained Transformer agents for these objectives: RL-WP for wins
and RL-Point for points \cite{guan2023koikoi,pinnedkoikoi}. We adapt their
released actors and build an endgame controller that uses public play to
improve their decisions. The central task is to connect opponent inference
to an executable continuation: a hidden-card search must share each learner
decision across indistinguishable deals and execute the policy it evaluated.

Our controller makes this connection in three steps. Eight public hand
discards reveal the opponent's initial hand. Reconstructing its earlier
network inputs identifies WP or Point in 86.1\% of natural final-round starts
(Section~\ref{sec:public-revelation}). Current-round action likelihoods then
filter the possible hidden hands. Finally, search retains complete contingent
policies and exact comparisons against the unchanged parent. A retained
improvement remains executable when the computation limit prevents further
search (Section~\ref{sec:incumbent-tail}).

Public outcome certificates make these programs smaller. Where the final-round
score and turn guarantee a win under every legal continuation, the program
calls its parent instead of storing each remaining decision. Search accounts
for that region's belief mass directly. Across all 512 saved sources,
this compression reduces controller planning transitions by 8.84\%, completes
five additional parent references and retains three additional improvements
under the same caps. Independent replay verifies the entire adopted programs
(Section~\ref{sec:symbolic-tail}).

A fixed comparison on 512 new source matches evaluates the explicit controller
against parent and root-only controls. Mean conditional gains over the parents
are 0.2619 and 0.3246 percentage points. The predefined replicated actual-win
criterion is not met. Reporting both quantities separates improvement under
the reconstructed model from the realized match outcomes.

The parent actors themselves improve through terminal-match self-play. We
convert the released action scores into a stochastic policy and optimize it
with PPO \cite{schulman2017ppo}, retaining value across round boundaries.
After 8.52 million additional decisions each, both adapted agents beat
assisted RL-WP on held-out duplicate deals with greedy action selection.
Starting from RL-Point instead raises equal-opponent mixture point margins
by 1.77--2.08 points over matched WP-initialized controls
(Section~\ref{sec:point-initialization}).

An exact two-step analysis explains a separate learning failure: switching
between players' critics reverses the expected update when their observations
contain different information. Returns between learner decisions correct the
complete shared-parameter example (Section~\ref{sec:observer}). Koi-Koi tests
of this construction and a fixed-viewer alternative establish no additional
replicated win gain.

\section{Related work}
\subsection{Learning to play Koi-Koi}
Sato and Ikeda compared policy gradients with Neural Fitted Q Iteration using
268 game features and a rule-based opponent \cite{sato2017koikoi}. Their
simplified scoring variant differs from the eight-round protocol used here.
Nagamine and Suzuki changed Monte Carlo rollout rewards to construct
fast-finishing and blocking strategies \cite{nagamine2021strategy}. With
koi-koi continuation disabled, they proposed switching scoring and
fast-finishing strategies across a match as future work. This anticipates the
motivation for combining objectives; our experiments include continuation
decisions and compare learned selection with fixed card/koi roles.

Takaoka et al. change UCT choices according to the current score to keep games
close and study player enjoyment \cite{takaoka2018fun}. Their objective-dependent
search complements the scoring and win-oriented approaches considered here.

Guan et al. introduced the phase-specific Transformers and reward objectives
that produce RL-WP and RL-Point \cite{guan2023koikoi}. We retain their released
representations and input conventions, then adapt an explicit sampling policy.
Their released RL-WP training code assigns round-ending targets from a
continuation-win table indexed by next round, score and dealer
\cite{pinnedkoikoi}. Our PPO objective uses match-end rewards and bootstraps across
round boundaries. The experiments measure the effect of this additional
optimization and its transfer across the two published opponents.

\subsection{Adapting action scores and exploration}
Policy distillation converts teacher action values into temperature-scaled
probabilities and trains a student to match them \cite{rusu2016distillation}.
We retain the released actor and optimize it directly with match rewards.
Discrete SAC derives categorical maximum-entropy learning
\cite{christodoulou2019discrete}, and target-entropy scheduling adapts exploration
as learning proceeds \cite{xu2021entropy}. Our continuation-temperature control
instead fixes score divisors before PPO training; the critic retains
ordinary match returns. We test one-head calibration
(Appendix~\ref{app:temperature}) and a four-head calibration when changing
from WP to Point initialization (Section~\ref{sec:point-initialization}).
These controls change both sampling probabilities and policy-loss derivatives.
They measure the transfer of published action scores into a match-trained
policy rather than introduce a new entropy-control algorithm.

\subsection{Self-play and strategic interaction}
Neural Fictitious Self-Play combines learned best responses with supervised
learning of average strategies \cite{heinrich2016nfsp}. Policy-Space Response
Oracles compute responses to population mixtures selected through an empirical
game \cite{lanctot2017psro}. Counterfactual regret minimization connects local
regret reduction to approximate equilibrium in two-player zero-sum games with
perfect recall \cite{zinkevich2007cfr}. Our current-policy self-play instead
optimizes an inherited actor and measures its head-to-head performance against
fixed opponents. Those measurements characterize matchups; exploitability
requires a separate analysis.

\subsection{Opponent inference and guided starting states}
Bayes' Bluff updates opponent-strategy beliefs from both showdown hands,
whose private cards become known, and folded hands, whose hidden cards must
be marginalized \cite{southey2005bayes}. Ganzfried et al. extend Bayesian
opponent modeling to multiplayer games with partial revelation, collecting
observations under a default strategy before switching to model-based
responses \cite{ganzfried2024opponent}. Thus delayed revelation and later
adaptation already have direct precedents.

Policy Based Inference uses action likelihoods under a player model to weight
possible hidden deals in Skat \cite{rebstock2019policy}. Our revelation study
instead reconstructs a completed Koi-Koi hand and the opponent's earlier
network inputs exactly, then eliminates inconsistent members of a fixed
deterministic policy set. The measured contribution is the resulting
identification coverage and a matched test of whether this information
improves subsequent play.

Bayesian Policy Reuse selects from learned policies using observed signals
about an unknown task instance \cite{rosman2016reuse}. DRON incorporates
opponent observations into a deep Q-network and learns opponent-dependent
responses \cite{he2016opponent}. Our public/masked comparison isolates the
effect of supplying the recovered opponent identity to the adapted actor.

Jump-Start Reinforcement Learning uses a guide policy to construct a curriculum
of starting states \cite{uchendu2023jump}. Our final-round experiment fixes
the guide for rounds 1--7 and trains from its naturally reached round-eight
states. A second comparison starts at the first fresh round after public
identification and trains through the remaining match. Both constructions
preserve the parent prefix before activation and isolate subsequent adaptation.
They apply guided learning to test the value of reconstructed public
information in Koi-Koi.

\subsection{Critic information and decision times}
Baisero and Amato analyze the bias from substituting state critics for history
critics and develop a history-state formulation \cite{baisero2022asymmetric}.
Lyu et al. show that state-based multi-agent critics can bias policy gradients
and, even when unbiased, can increase their variance \cite{lyu2022critics}.
Our example studies a specific instance of this conditioning problem: a
player-relative return crosses between two players' observations. It makes the
failure explicit with exact conditional values and a policy update that reduces
true expected payoff. Its return recurrence is checked in all three simulator
backends.

Sutton, Precup and Singh formalize temporally extended decisions with options
and semi-Markov models \cite{sutton1999options}. Learner-decision returns use a
related temporal organization: opponent moves lie inside an interval between
the learner's actions. Our analysis focuses on the information used at those
boundaries, with exactness established for the finite example and Koi-Koi
assessed in the fixed-opponent comparison.

\subsection{Search and rollout-based improvement}
Information Set Monte Carlo Tree Search organizes search around information
sets \cite{cowling2012ismcts}; ReBeL combines learning and search through public
belief states \cite{brown2020rebel}. Expert Iteration alternates search-based
improvement with learning a policy that generalizes its decisions
\cite{anthony2017expert}. Our complete-match rollout-search comparison uses MLP continuation policies
that receive only acting-player observations inside sampled worlds; it did not
replicate a match-level benefit. Later diagnostics apply retained Transformers
to paired stop/continue and card-action rollouts, with fresh confirmation
samples for proposed changes. These diagnostics extend the implementation
coverage; their selected roots do not constitute a match-level comparison.

Exact planning and baseline improvement provide complementary precedents.
HSVI returns an anytime POMDP policy together with a bound on its regret
\cite{smith2004hsvi}. SPIBB studies improvement from fixed data without access
to the true environment, using the baseline where uncertainty is high
\cite{laroche2019spibb}; MCTS-SPIBB computes local online responses in that
framework \cite{castellini2023mcts}.

Sim\~{a}o et al. extend SPIBB to POMDPs through finite-state controllers
\cite{simao2023pomdpspi}. They estimate a history MDP from offline trajectories
and bound the possible performance loss with high probability, assuming that
finite histories preserve the required transition and reward information.
Their guarantee accounts for uncertainty from finite data.

Our endgame controller takes the game rules and identified opponent model as
given, and constructs a finite hidden-card belief. Its guarantee conditions on
that model and deal law. It compares completed policies exactly and keeps
the parent when a strict gain cannot be established. The domain contribution is
the path from reconstructed opponent inputs to verified executable policies,
with explicit computation limits and complete-match assessment.

Suzuki's Open Hanafuda AI provides a recent public implementation combining
simulation with observable-information inference and heuristics
\cite{suzuki2026openhanafuda}. Its declaration and scoring rules differ from
our pinned eight-round benchmark, so its internal Hard-versus-Normal results
do not provide a head-to-head comparison with these agents.

Learning action choices from rollout outcomes is also established. Lagoudakis
and Parr formulate policy iteration as classification
\cite{lagoudakis2003classification}; Dimitrakakis and Lagoudakis improve rollout
sample allocation \cite{dimitrakakis2008rollout}. Langford and Zadrozny relate
policy performance to reward-sensitive classification
\cite{langford2005classification}. DAgger collects observations influenced by the
learner's actions \cite{ross2011dagger}, and AggreVaTe incorporates expert
cost-to-go information in interactive learning \cite{ross2014aggrevate}.
These iterative methods address the distribution induced by policy updates.
Our fixed-source regression tests one fitting stage before complete-match
evaluation. At each expert disagreement, both proposals share hidden cards
and future deals. Common random numbers can reduce relative-utility variance
under suitable coupling \cite{yadav2026common}; the generated data records the
resulting covariance.

\subsection{Policy composition and evaluation}
Comanici and Precup learn policy switching through option termination conditions
\cite{comanici2010switching}. We select between expert action proposals at each
decision, using phase-only selectors and fixed card/koi hybrids as controls.
The domain question is whether point-oriented and win-oriented policies supply
useful alternatives for match optimization.

Agarwal et al. show why few-run reinforcement-learning results need careful
uncertainty reporting \cite{agarwal2021precipice}. We retain both trained
models, exchange seats on common deals and bootstrap complete deal pairs.
Development and held-out confirmation use distinct deals. These intervals
measure deal variation; two model seeds offer limited evidence about future
training-run variation.

\section{Rules, observations, and comparator}
\subsection{Versioned game protocols}
The primary protocol, \texttt{paper8}, follows the pinned public implementation:
at most eight rounds, 30 initial points per player, and termination when either
player's points reach zero or below. The separate \texttt{research12} protocol
uses twelve rounds and disables bankruptcy termination. Other round rules
are shared. Results from these protocols are not interchangeable.

A round deals eight cards to each hand, eight to the field, and leaves 24 in
the stock. A player discards, resolves a matching capture, reveals a stock
card, resolves that capture, and may be offered stop/continue after an
increase in scored combinations. Forced transitions are folded into the
simulator step. In the pinned scoring convention there is no seven-point
doubling or opponent-koi doubling. After an exhausted round without a scoring
stop, the initial dealer receives one point. The repository's versioned
\texttt{docs/RULES.md} supplies the complete scoring contract, including
nonstandard combinations inherited from the comparator.

The action space has 48 card actions and two stop/continue actions. Decision
phases are discard, capture from the hand play, capture from the stock reveal,
and koi. The two capture phases share an actor. Policies receive their own
hand and public state/history; the opponent's hand, stock order, random-number
state, and future events are excluded. A legal-action mask is applied before
sampling or greedy selection.

\subsection{Pinned opponents and public assistance}
We pin the released source and load the original RL-WP and RL-Point
weights without further training. The reproducibility manifest identifies
the revision and all six weight files. The comparison uses the public
endgame guard corresponding to upstream assistance for both sides. This guard
restricts actions when a public match-outcome decision is solved, including
stopping with a winning final-round score. It does not inspect hidden cards.

The primary opponent is assisted RL-WP. Assisted RL-Point is a distinct
secondary opponent, because point-oriented and win-oriented training need not
produce the same ordering. All final match evaluations use strict CPU
inference and greedy actions. GPU feature/inference checks support training
compatibility but do not establish universal floating-point action identity.

\section{Self-play adaptation}
\subsection{Policy and value functions}
We initialize the three phase networks from the original RL-WP weights.
Their inherited raw action scores $q_\theta(o,a)$ are not probabilities.
For the legal action set $\mathcal A(o)$, training samples from
\begin{equation}
 \pi_{\theta,T}(a\mid o)=
 \frac{\exp(q_\theta(o,a)/T)}
 {\sum_{b\in\mathcal A(o)}\exp(q_\theta(o,b)/T)},\qquad a\in\mathcal A(o).
\end{equation}
The public endgame guard can further narrow $\mathcal A(o)$. At evaluation,
the agent chooses a maximizing legal score, so a positive temperature does not
change the mathematical argmax for fixed scores. Temperature can nevertheless
change the learned policy through sampling and optimization. Dropout remains
disabled during both collection and gradient updates, preserving a consistent
policy for stored log probabilities.

The inherited actors retain the upstream 256-wide, two-layer Transformer
architecture. Native features reproduce the original 300-by-50 representation,
including its historical input conventions. A separate value network receives
a 560-dimensional observation of its own hand and public state, using two
256-unit tanh hidden
layers followed by a scalar output. Its output layer is initialized to zero;
it shares no actor parameters. Both actor and critic use only information available to the acting player.

\subsection{Player-relative returns}
Both players use the same current stochastic policy during self-play. Rewards
are zero until match termination, when the acting player receives $+1$, $0$,
or $-1$ for a win, draw, or loss. We use an undiscounted, player-relative
adaptation of generalized advantage estimation \cite{schulman2015gae}.
Let $d_t$ indicate match termination and let $s_t$ be $+1$ when consecutive
decision states have the same acting player and $-1$ otherwise. Then
\begin{align}
 \delta_t &= r_t+(1-d_t)s_t V(o_{t+1})-V(o_t),\\
 A_t &= \delta_t+(1-d_t)s_t\lambda A_{t+1},\qquad \lambda=0.95.
\end{align}
Rollout truncation bootstraps from the next observation. Round boundaries
retain continuation value unless the match ends. A terminal transition masks
the value of the automatically reset next match. The sign follows the actual
next acting player, which need not alternate at every decision phase.

Advantages are normalized over each rollout batch before optimization.
We optimize the clipped PPO surrogate with clipping parameter 0.2, add
one-half the value mean-squared error, and subtract 0.02 times policy entropy.
The global trainable gradient norm is clipped at 0.5. Adam uses
$\epsilon=10^{-5}$ and separate actor/value learning-rate groups. The first
20 updates freeze actor parameters and fit only the critic; the next 500
updates train both. This warmup is part of the reported budget.

\subsection{Fixed development comparison}
Each update collects 128 decisions from each of 128 parallel environments and
uses three minibatch epochs with minibatch size 512. Each model therefore
receives exactly 8,519,680 simulator decisions in 520 updates. The value
learning rate is $10^{-4}$. The warm setting uses $T=0.05$ and actor learning
rate $3\times10^{-6}$; the cold setting uses $T=0.01$ and
$6\times10^{-7}$. Both settings are trained at seeds 6710001 and 6720001.
Only the final update-520 checkpoints enter the comparison. Intermediate
snapshots are retained for accounting and recovery, not strength selection.

The comparison measures the joint effect of temperature and actor learning rate,
with their ratio held fixed. The settings can produce different sampled
trajectories, entropy and adaptive optimizer states. Execution is eager float32
with TF32 disabled. The GPU is an
NVIDIA GeForce RTX 5070 Ti; the runtime records PyTorch 2.12.0+cu130 and NumPy
2.5.4. Evaluation is kept separate from training.

\subsection{Implementation and inference cost}
A shared C++/CUDA transition core runs the same rules on CPU and GPU. Differential
checks compare transitions and observation features with the Python reference and
published model inputs; checkpoints retain the complete state for exact restart.
The adapted actor retains greedy, search-free deployment. On a Ryzen 9 9900X
with two CPU threads, the canonical model averages 1.585 ms per cached-state
decision, including feature construction and public assistance. The original
RL-WP agent averages 1.974 ms on the same corpus. Appendix~\ref{app:systems}
reports the validation corpus, separate training budgets and timing procedure.

\section{Evaluation and selection}
\subsection{Duplicate deals and uncertainty}
For each seed we play two matches with candidate/opponent seats exchanged,
retaining the same seat-assigned deal streams and initial dealer. If the
candidate's two match scores are $w_{i0},w_{i1}\in\{0,1/2,1\}$, the observation
for inference is $x_i=(w_{i0}+w_{i1})/2$. The reported draw-adjusted win score
is $\bar x$. We resample whole pairs, not individual match legs, for 5,000
bootstrap replicates using random seed 99 and linearly interpolated 2.5th and
97.5th percentiles. Paired method comparisons resample the per-deal differences.
The intervals quantify deal variation for each fixed model. Results for the
two training seeds are reported separately.

Point difference is final candidate points minus opponent points, averaged
across the two seats. Because total points remain 60, this is twice the
candidate's final-points-minus-initial-30 statistic used in the original paper.
Positive expected points are reported separately and cannot substitute for a
win-score criterion. Shared deals across models or opponents do not create
additional independent deal samples.

\subsection{Development and frozen final rules}
The development study uses 5,000 duplicate pairs with seeds
7,200,000--7,204,999 for the initial policy and four trained endpoints. An arm
qualifies only if both training seeds have an absolute win-score 95\% interval
lower bound above 0.5. A cold-over-warm benefit requires positive lower bounds
for both paired score differences. Selection chooses cold if it qualifies and
shows that replicated benefit; otherwise it chooses qualified warm, then
qualified cold, and otherwise no method. The canonical model always uses
training seed 6710001 of the selected arm. There is no best-seed ranking.

The selection rules were fixed before this study's outcomes. Earlier development
explored other methods and included an inconclusive two-seed warm screen at
1,000 pairs. The present comparison uses fresh training seeds and 5,000 pairs;
a separate final test provides confirmation after this development process.

The frozen final test evaluates both selected models against assisted RL-WP
and, separately, assisted RL-Point. Each of four jobs plays 10,000 duplicate
pairs on seeds 100,000,000--100,009,999: 80,000 matches on 10,000 unique deal
streams. The primary criterion requires both RL-WP interval lower bounds to
exceed 0.5. The secondary RL-Point criterion is recorded separately. There is
no change to the selected checkpoints or comparison budget during this final test.
All four jobs run to completion. Four two-thread CPU workers run concurrently;
a separate experiment measures isolated inference latency.

\subsection{Point initialization: points and wins separate}
\label{sec:point-initialization}
Changing the initial policy offers another route to adaptation. At each of two
fresh seeds, we train three arms: original WP with divisor 0.05, original Point
with divisor 0.05, and original Point with calibrated phase-specific divisors.
Each receives the same 20 critic-only and 500 policy updates, or 8,519,680
decisions. Within a seed, initial critic, optimizer, environment and random
states match; the two Point arms also start from identical actor parameters.
Deployment uses the unchanged greedy policy and public endgame assistance.

Calibration matches Point's mean normalized action entropy to WP at 0.05 on
75 source groups from an existing random-play input corpus. The other 25 groups
are reserved for descriptive validation. Divisors are fixed before training;
no match outcomes enter calibration. This controls one property of the initial
sampling policy, while also changing policy-loss derivatives.

The six endpoints and two retained agents play 131,072 matches on 8,192 shared
development deals. The primary criterion requires calibrated Point to improve
equal-mixture win score over both WP-fixed and retained controls at both seeds.

Calibrated-minus-fixed Point mixture wins change by $+0.7385$ and $-0.3387$

\section{Critic conditioning across players}
\label{sec:observer}
Correct payoff signs do not ensure that the next acting player's observation
contains the information needed by the current learner's target. The following
finite game isolates this issue without critic fitting or sampled trajectories.

A learner privately observes a uniform bit $X$ and takes a public binary action
$A$, with $\pi(A=1\mid X=x)=\sigma(c_x\theta)$, where $c_0=1$ and
$c_1=-1/100$. Both signal-dependent policies share the scalar parameter $\theta$.
The opponent sees $A$, takes one forced action, and the game ends with learner
payoff $R\in\{-1,+1\}$.
Define $\Pr(R=+1\mid X=x,A=a)=(1+\mu_{xa})/2$, where
\begin{equation}
 (\mu_{xa})=\begin{pmatrix}0.20&0.21\\1&-1\end{pmatrix}.
\end{equation}
At $\theta=0$, the exact learner values are
$v_0=0.205,v_1=0$. The opponent's exact own-payoff value is $-m_A$, with
$m_0=0.60,m_1=-0.395$. Signed GAE at the learner action therefore gives
\begin{equation}
 \widehat A=\lambda R+(1-\lambda)m_A-v_X.
 \label{eq:observer-advantage}
\end{equation}
For the objective $J=\mathbb E[R]$ and $\lambda=0.95$, direct differentiation
and exact enumeration give
\begin{align}
 J'(0)&=\frac{3}{800}=0.00375,\\
 \mathbb E[\widehat A\,\partial_\theta\log\pi(A\mid X)]
 &=-\frac{8301}{3200000}=-0.0025940625.
\end{align}
The expected update has the opposite sign to the true gradient
(Figure~\ref{fig:observer-reversal}). A sufficiently small positive step
in that direction therefore reduces true expected payoff. The opponent critic
averages over $X$, changing the relative targets for the two actions when the
learner knows $X=0$. This result concerns the population score-function update
before PPO clipping and empirical advantage normalization.

\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.88\linewidth]{generated/observer_reversal.pdf}
\caption{Exact expectations in the two-step game. The true gradient is positive,
but opponent-observation GAE points in the opposite direction for
$\lambda<6567/6767\approx0.9704$. Both critics are exact for their respective
observations. The marked $\lambda=0.95$ case therefore reduces expected payoff
under a sufficiently small positive update.}
\label{fig:observer-reversal}
\end{figure}

Three controls recover the true expectation for the complete game: $\lambda=1$, a
single learner-to-terminal transition, and an intermediate critic conditioned
on both $X$ and $A$. The Python, native CPU and CUDA implementations reproduce
the return calculation. The scalar expectation also agrees with direct objective
differentiation. Appendix~\ref{app:observer} gives the derivation and a
learner-decision implementation with explicit tail masks.
The Koi-Koi match results above use the original signed estimator.
Appendix~\ref{sec:learner} compares the return constructions under fixed-opponent
training.

The $\lambda=1$ cancellation requires reaching termination. Cutting this same
game after the learner action leaves the opponent-observation bootstrap and
a negative expected update, even at $\lambda=1$. Retaining the learner history
restores the correct gradient. Appendix~\ref{app:trace} separates the internal
bootstrap terms from this rollout-boundary term.

\subsection{Directional measurements in the retained agents}
\label{sec:directional}
To connect the finite example to actual play, we collect 4,096 complete sampled
self-play matches from each retained agent, sharing initial deals across the
two agents. For a fixed public feature $f$, the local policy
$\pi_\theta(a\mid o)\propto\pi_0(a\mid o)\exp(\theta f(o,a))$ has score
$f(o,a)-\mathbb E_{\pi_0}f(o,A)$. The five features are a continuation indicator
and one base-probability preference feature for each decision phase. Features
and critics stay fixed. We sum score times advantage separately for each seat,
then average the two unilateral responses against the frozen other seat.

Complete terminal Monte Carlo contributions are compared with full-match
GAE0.95 and 128-step-window GAE0.95/GAE1, using independently assigned cutoff
positions. One 70-coordinate simultaneous bootstrap family covers all means
and differences across both agents. Every Monte Carlo mean and every paired
difference interval includes zero. Thus these probes do not establish the
finite example's reversal in the retained agents. The measurement concerns
these local policy directions before PPO clipping and normalization; it does
not identify observer mismatch separately from critic approximation error.
All 8,192 matches are replayed on native CPU, and independent scalar score and
return calculations agree to $8.9\times10^{-16}$. Full coordinates and the
fixed design are in \path{docs/DIRECTIONAL_RETURN_RESULTS.rst}.

\subsection{Do return repairs improve learned play?}
The exact counterexample motivates two implementations: returns between
learner decisions, and a critic that maintains each player's viewpoint through
all primitive decisions. Both are tested from the two retained parents against
unchanged parents and signed-return controls. Each new arm receives

full matches per study. The fixed-observer contrast holds critic fitting fixed;

Learner-decision returns have positive mean win changes in all eight primary

The fixed-observer comparison has no positive lower bound among its eight

and~\ref{sec:observer-selfplay} retain the complete training protocols,

\section{Information recovered from public play}
\label{sec:public-information}
The fixed-observer critic uses 561 viewer-relative features
(Appendix~\ref{sec:observer-selfplay}). Public history supports two concrete
reconstructions. A viewer-seat bit makes
the critic representation distinguish actor inputs that it previously merged.
Completed hand discards reveal enough information to reconstruct an opponent's
earlier inputs and identify it within a fixed model class. We verify these
information properties before testing their use in learning.

\subsection{Aligning actor and critic inputs}
The actor and critic also differ in their input representations. The published
actor records the absolute dealer seat, whereas the 561-input critic records
dealer status relative to its viewer. Exchanging seat labels preserves the
critic input but changes two actor feature rows. On 256 retained source
decisions per parent, this relabeling changes 4 and 5 greedy actions,
respectively. All nine changes occur at discard decisions. Legal actions and
public endgame assistance are unchanged, and the actor features agree with
the pinned upstream implementation on all 1,024 original/relabeled probes.

A public viewer-seat bit resolves this ambiguity. With that bit and the
relative-dealer feature, an observation-only decoder recovers the absolute
dealer and exactly reconstructs the complete $300\times50$ actor input from
the 562-element critic row on all 1,024 probes. The probes span discard,
hand-capture, draw-capture and stop/continue decisions. Reconstruction uses
only that active viewer's features, without stepping the saved states or
running the actor. This diagnoses a concrete actor--critic information
mismatch and supplies an explicit repair.

\subsection{Retaining earlier decision information}
\label{sec:temporal-information}
We next test whether later critic features retain the input and action of an
earlier decision. An observation-only decoder receives a later 562-element
row and the public round, turn and phase of
an earlier decision by the same viewer, and reconstructs that decision's actor
input and chosen action. The viewer's initial hand is recovered from its
remaining cards and recorded hand plays. Starting from the recorded initial
field, the decoder replays public card, capture and continuation events up to
the requested address. It receives no simulator state, hidden opponent hand,
stock order, earlier observation or policy output.

All 3,764 chronological pairs within the same played round in the saved
trajectories match exactly: 1,864 pairs for the first parent and 1,900 for the
second. In 1,968 pairs the later actor is the opponent. The checks cover all
four earlier decision phases and separations up to 19 decisions. They reuse
the 512 source positions and add no simulator steps or policy inference.

For a fixed differentiable policy, the score
$\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(a\mid o)$ is determined by its input and chosen
action. Recovering both arguments at a supplied public address determines the
earlier score on these checked pairs. This is measured within-round retention, rather than a
proof for every trajectory or across round boundaries. Stop actions end a round
and have no same-round successor. Appendix~\ref{sec:seat-comparison} tests whether this representation repair
improves playing strength.

\begin{samepage}
\subsection{Recovering opponent information after a hand is revealed}
\label{sec:public-revelation}
A player's eight hand cards are discarded one by one; stock draws never enter
the hand. Once all eight hand discards have been observed, their set reveals
that round's initial hand. Combining it with the public board, captures and
action history reconstructs the player's earlier inputs. This reconstruction
becomes available after the eighth discard, including when that action ends
the round. It cannot be used to choose any earlier action.
\end{samepage}

We retain the two fixed assisted CPU opponents as candidate models. At each
revelation, we reconstruct their inputs at earlier opponent decisions and
remove any candidate whose predicted action disagrees with the observed one.
The candidate set carries across rounds. An unresolved set remains unresolved;
an empty set records model mismatch. The true opponent label is used only for
scoring the diagnostic, never for prediction.

Across all 2,048 retained training prefixes, the procedure identifies the true
opponent in 1,746 cases and leaves 302 unresolved. It makes no incorrect
identification or exclusion of the true model. At the 1,856 natural final-round
starts, 1,598 have an identified opponent (86.1\%;
Table~\ref{tab:public-revelation}). All 36,977 reconstructed historical inputs
and assistance masks agree with the original upstream builders. Independent
replay verifies the time of each revelation and every candidate exclusion.
These prefixes share 512 deals; they are not 2,048 independent full matches.

\section{Limitations and interpretation}
The comparisons measure head-to-head performance under the pinned Koi-Koi
rules. The exact planner's guarantee requires the evaluated belief to match
the conditional deal and opponent model. Conditional improvement certificates
do not by themselves determine population win-rate gains. The prospective
comparison has 128 distinct deal streams; it does not resolve sub-percentage-
point effects reliably.

Two training seeds and fixed opponents limit generality: held-out deals measure
endpoint performance, not robustness across optimization runs. The ledger
retains unsuccessful experiments. Exploitability and human performance are
unmeasured. The original warm/cold comparison changes temperature and learning
rate jointly. Latency covers one machine and a cached random-play workload.

\section{Conclusion}
Public reconstruction identifies WP or Point in 86.1\% of natural final-round
starts and supports exact comparisons of executable endgame policies.
Search retains complete contingent improvements under computation caps.
Public score certificates compress these programs into explicit decisions and
checked parent continuations. On the same 512 saved sources, compression
reduces controller planning transitions by 8.84\% and raises adopted improvements
from 83 to 86. Every realized win score and final point margin remains unchanged
from the preceding controller. Certified planning coverage increases; an
additional match-win gain remains unconfirmed.

The exact shared-parameter example identifies a separate critic-conditioning
failure: switching to the opponent's observation can reverse the expected
policy update. Learner-decision returns correct the complete example. Their
Koi-Koi tests establish no replicated additional win gain.

Terminal-match self-play improves the released Koi-Koi win policy at both
adapted seeds while preserving its Transformer actor and greedy inference.
Point initialization also raises mixture point margins by 1.77--2.08 points
over matched WP controls. These measured adaptation gains provide the parent
actors for the public-information controller.

\section{Observer-bootstrap calculation and learner-decision returns}
\label{app:observer}
\subsection{Exact terminal-game calculation}
At the uniform policy, $v_x=(\mu_{x0}+\mu_{x1})/2$ and
$m_a=(\mu_{0a}+\mu_{1a})/2$. The learner's immediate reward is zero and the
opponent's terminal reward is $-R$. The first residual is $m_A-v_X$; the second
is $m_A-R$. Applying the negative player-transition sign yields
$(m_A-v_X)-\lambda(m_A-R)$, proving
Equation~\ref{eq:observer-advantage}.

First parameterize the two logits independently as $\theta_0,\theta_1$.
Critic targets are detached in the policy update. The score for parameter $\theta_x$ is
$\mathbf1\{X=x\}(A-1/2)$. Since $X$ is uniform,
\begin{equation}
 g_x(\lambda)=\frac{\lambda(\mu_{x1}-\mu_{x0})
 +(1-\lambda)(m_1-m_0)}{8}.
\end{equation}
The true gradient is $(1/800,-1/4)$, with first component $0.00125$.
Signed GAE at $\lambda=0.95$ gives $g_0=-161/32000=-0.00503125$ and
$g_1=-0.24371875$. The first component is positive exactly
when $\lambda>199/201$. These are exact expectations over the finite game,
not estimated confidence intervals. The single-transition and $\lambda=1$
controls use $R-v_X$; conditioning the intermediate critic on $(X,A)$ also
restores the expectation. The first-component sample variance is about
0.1197453 for the single transition versus 0.1080973 for signed GAE at $\lambda=0.95$.
The correction therefore changes variance as well as bias.

Now restrict the logits to $(\theta,-\alpha\theta)$ for fixed $\alpha$.
The scalar score is $(A-1/2)c_X$ with $c=(1,-\alpha)$. The chain rule gives
\begin{align}
 J'(0)&=\frac{1+200\alpha}{800},\\
 g_{\rm shared}(\lambda)&=g_0(\lambda)-\alpha g_1(\lambda)
 =\frac{201(1+\alpha)\lambda-199(1-\alpha)}{1600}.
\end{align}
For $\alpha=1/100$ and $\lambda=19/20$, these are $3/800$ and
$-8301/3200000$. The true objective's directional derivative along the expected
update is their product:
\begin{equation}
 J'(0)\,g_{\rm shared}(19/20)=-\frac{24903}{2560000000}<0.
\end{equation}
The positive-sign threshold
is $\lambda>6567/6767$. All three controls recover $3/800$. This establishes
local descent in the stated shared-parameter family; the independently
parameterized policy above has only one reversed coordinate.

The executable diagnostic is \path{scripts/diagnose_observer_bootstrap.py}.
It enumerates all signal/action/payoff combinations, including zero-probability
outcomes, checks the conditional critic means and compares analytic gradients
with the return implementations to a gradient tolerance of $3\times10^{-8}$.
The evidence exporter verifies both retained
CPU/CUDA reports, their source identities and the rational calculation. No
Koi-Koi matches or policy updates contribute to this example.
The shared-parameter extension, \path{scripts/diagnose_shared_observer_bootstrap.py},
uses rational outcome enumeration and direct scalar autograd. It reruns the
Python/native CPU calculation and projects the pinned earlier CPU/CUDA gradients
by $(1,-1/100)$; it performs no additional CUDA computation. The evidence ledger
binds both calculations and their exact source files.

\subsection{Internal and boundary bootstrap terms}
\label{app:trace}
The standard GAE expansion \cite{schulman2015gae} identifies the effect of
an observer change at any rollout position. Fix a root action by seat $i$,
use $\gamma=1$, and express rewards $r_k$ and values in that seat's payoff
units. If $p_k$ is the active seat, write
$W_k=(-1)^{i+p_k}V_k^{p_k}$ and let $G_k$ be the realized return through match
termination, including any suffix beyond the $n$-step rollout. Thus
$G_k=r_k+G_{k+1}$. Consecutive player-change signs telescope to $(-1)^{i+p_k}$,
including repeated actions by the same player. With $e_k=W_k-G_k$,
\begin{align}
 \widehat A_\lambda
 &=\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\lambda^k(r_k+W_{k+1}-W_k)\\
 &=G_0-W_0+(1-\lambda)\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}\lambda^{k-1}e_k
   +\lambda^{n-1}e_n.\label{eq:trace-error}
\end{align}
This pathwise identity follows by substituting $r_k=G_k-G_{k+1}$ and
collecting coefficients; $0^0=1$. A terminal boundary has $W_n=G_n=0$.
A nonterminal cutoff generally retains $e_n$, including when $\lambda=1$.

Let $S_0=\nabla\log\pi_i(a_0\mid H_0^i)$, hold the opponent policy fixed,
and treat targets as constants. If $W_0$ is action-independent given the
root policy information, multiplying the last two terms of
Equation~\ref{eq:trace-error} by $S_0$ and taking expectations gives the
error in this root score contribution. Exactness conditional on the active
opponent's observation need not make $\mathbb E[S_0e_k]$ zero. In contrast,
let $U_k=\mathbb E[G_k\mid H_k^i]$ use the root player's full history,
including its root action. When $S_0$ is measurable in $H_k^i$,
\begin{equation}
 \mathbb E[S_0(U_k-G_k)]
 =\mathbb E[S_0\,\mathbb E[U_k-G_k\mid H_k^i]]=0.
\end{equation}
The same argument applies at a nonterminal cutoff and requires no opponent
hand. Finite feature vectors need not retain this history. The calculation
concerns one player's score contribution; updating both seats of a shared
zero-sum policy does not turn their identically zero total payoff into a
joint improvement objective.

For the scalar example, cut the rollout immediately after the learner action.
Exact enumeration gives
\begin{align}
 \mathbb E[S_0(m_A-\mu_{XA})]&=\TraceScoreCovariance,\\
 g_{\rm cutoff}(\lambda)&=\TraceSignedCutoff,\qquad 0\leq\lambda\leq1.
\end{align}
The true gradient and the exact learner-history update are both $3/800$.
The diagnostic
\path{scripts/diagnose_observer_trace_identity.py} verifies the pathwise
identity in \TraceExactCases{} rational cases and all 16 combinations of
terminal/cutoff, return construction and tested $\lambda$ values. CPU returns
agree within $1.513\times10^{-9}$. This establishes the cutoff failure in the
example; attributing a Koi-Koi performance difference to that mechanism
requires a separate controlled comparison.

\subsection{Learner-decision implementation}

The companion \path{scripts/learner_returns.py} accumulates fixed-opponent
transitions from a learner action to its next observation or match termination.
It uses learner-relative rewards, including opponent-caused terminal rewards,
and $\lambda^k$ eligibility decay across $k$ primitive decisions. At a rollout
boundary, a final learner action without a next learner observation or terminal
outcome is excluded from both policy and value losses. Its starting value still
bootstraps the preceding completed transition. Reset masks block rewards from
the next match. Simulator work, learner actions, valid loss rows and omitted
tails are counted separately.

The integrated PPO variant also removes the opponent critic and normalizes
value loss over valid learner rows, changing joint clipping and minibatch sizes.
These are part of the full intervention. The fixed comparison in
\path{docs/LEARNER_RETURN_STUDY.rst} includes the original signed estimator and
$\lambda=1$ controls; Appendix~\ref{sec:learner} reports the completed results.
Finite current observations and boundary-dependent exclusions remain part of
the implemented estimator. The exact example concerns its stated finite game;
the Koi-Koi comparisons test the full learning procedures, including their

\section{Fixed-observer self-play}
\label{sec:observer-selfplay}
\subsection{Returns with a consistent viewer}
The finite-game analysis suggests retaining the same player's information at
each bootstrap. We implement this in self-play by evaluating two separate
critic rows at every primitive decision. Each row contains its viewer's own
cards and public information, plus a bit indicating whether that viewer is
acting. A shared MLP maps the resulting 561 features to $v_t^i$, the value for
viewer $i$. It processes rows independently; neither row contains the other
player's hand or the stock order. The actor and its input are unchanged.

Let $r_t$ denote seat zero's terminal-match reward and $c_t$ indicate that the
match continues after decision $t$. For each viewer $i\in\{0,1\}$, use
\begin{align}
 \delta_t^i &= (-1)^i r_t+c_t v_{t+1}^i-v_t^i,\\
 \widehat A_t^i &= \delta_t^i+\lambda c_t\widehat A_{t+1}^i.
\end{align}
A round ending leaves $c_t=1$; a match ending sets it to zero. At a rollout
cutoff, the recurrence ends with $\widehat A_H^i=0$ and retains each viewer's
own $v_H^i$ bootstrap. Fit $v_t^i+\widehat A_t^i$ for both viewers and use the
acting viewer's advantage for the policy. The two conditional values need not
sum to zero: their observations contain different private information.

The matched signed-return control uses the same two-row critic targets,
architecture, optimizer, minibatches and clipping. It changes only the policy
advantage, selecting active-viewer values for the historical signed recurrence.
Both arms use $\lambda=0.95$ and the warm adaptation settings. For each parent,
they start from the same retained actor and padded critic; all saved model,
optimizer, environment and RNG states agree after the 20
critic-only warmup updates. Each arm then performs 500 policy updates, for
8,519,680 simulator decisions and 17,039,360 value rows in total. This separates
the policy-return contrast from changes in critic fitting.

\section{Expert-controller protocols}
\label{app:controllers}
\subsection{PPO controller and matched continuation}
The controller uses two frozen experts: the adapted RL-WP parent and original
RL-Point. For acting-player observation $o$, let their legal proposals be
$a_W(o)$ and $a_P(o)$, after the same public endgame guard. The controller
acts on the distinct proposal set
\begin{equation}
 \mathcal C(o)=\{a_W(o),a_P(o)\},\qquad
 \mu_\phi(a\mid o)=\operatorname{softmax}_{a\in\mathcal C(o)}g_\phi(o,a).
\end{equation}
At agreement states, $\mathcal C(o)$ has one action and the policy
log-probability has zero gradient. Trainable choices occur only at disagreements.

The full controller receives the 560 observation features and one-hot encodings
of the two proposals, followed by two 128-unit tanh layers. Its initial
probability of the parent proposal is 0.9 at disagreements, so initial greedy
play selects the frozen parent expert proposal. Both experts remain frozen; only the controller
and an observation critic are trained with terminal-match PPO. The critic starts
from the parent's value network.

The fixed-budget study compares this controller with a phase-only
linear selector and ordinary continued Transformer PPO. Each of two parent
models initializes all three arms. Every arm receives 20 critic-only and 500
policy updates, or 8,519,680 additional simulator decisions. Only final
endpoints are evaluated, on 1,000 new duplicate deals against RL-WP, RL-Point,
and the corresponding parent. Both unchanged parents also play the published
opponents on these deals. Full versus phase changes capacity as well as
information; additional ablations are needed to isolate particular context
features. The full protocol and implementation are available in
\path{docs/ARBITRATION.md}.

\subsection{Paired-outcome regression}
A second implementation replaces PPO credit assignment with direct comparisons
at expert disagreements. Under a fixed behavior mixture (90\% parent, 10\%
Point), the simulator copies the current world into two branches. Each branch
executes one expert proposal; both players then follow the frozen parent to
match termination. Continuation policies see only their own observations.
The branches share hidden cards and future-round deal streams. For root-player
returns $G_W,G_P\in\{-1,0,1\}$, the regression target is
\begin{equation}
  \delta=G_P-G_W, \qquad
  \mathcal{L}(\phi)=\mathbb{E}[(h_\phi(o,a_W,a_P)-\delta)^2].
\end{equation}
The controller selects the proposal with the higher predicted return, with
native action-index tie breaking. Conditional on its observation, the target
averages the difference over hidden worlds encountered by the behavior policy.
Training exports observations and outcomes; simulator state stays outside the
learned controller.

The fixed study collects 65,536 disagreement roots per parent and fits the
controller for 20 epochs. A matched ablation removes explicit round and score
inputs while preserving architecture, data and initialization. Entire source
matches are held out for validation. Diagnostics report prediction error and
the observed one-action gain on these held-out roots, with uncertainty clustered
by source match. Repeated deployment is evaluated separately in full matches.
Generation decisions, branch completions and fitting work are counted separately;
this method incurs rollout computation beyond the PPO training budget.

Full-versus-ablation win intervals include zero in all four comparisons.

The targets also expose a learning difficulty: only about 9.6\% of roots
have different terminal win outcomes for the two proposals. On held-out
source matches, both full controllers have slightly higher prediction MSE
than predicting zero. Direct access to paired outcomes therefore does not by
itself provide a selector that improves repeated match play. Appendix controls
examine representation, data volume, regularization and continuation opponents.
Protocols are in \texttt{docs/PAIRED\_ROLLOUTS.md}.
